## **2. How its safety scoring works**

This is probably the most interesting part.

The application calculates a score out of 100 using several factors:

- Open places along the route: 25 points.
- Open help points nearby: 15 points.
- Distance on main roads: 15 points.
- Open transport locations: 10 points.
- Fewer gaps between open places: 15 points.
- Fewer turns: 10 points.
- Other scoring factors make up the remaining points.

For example, the demonstration compares:

**Safest route**

# **63/100**

Rank 1

- 12 minutes
- 4.7 km
- 50 places listed as open

  **Fastest route**
  # **27/100**
  Rank 2
- 10 minutes
- Lower estimated night activity

The user sacrifices two minutes to take a route with more nearby activity.

**Important distinction:** The application itself explicitly says its Night Activity Score is not a safety rating. More activity does not necessarily mean fewer incidents.

## **4. What I think about the idea**

Strong real-world problem

I genuinely think this is a meaningful application idea, especially in India.

Imagine someone travelling from a metro station to their PG at 10:30 PM.

Google Maps might recommend a route that is:

- 8 minutes shorter.
- Through an isolated road.
- Without shops or public activity.

A safety-oriented navigation system could recommend a slightly longer route with better infrastructure and more nearby assistance.

That's a genuinely useful distinction.

# **Rebuilding the architecture: Women's Urban Safety Intelligence Platform**

I would approach this as a product and engineering redesign, not simply an upgrade to the existing navigation application.

The opportunity is much bigger than finding safer streets.

**The goal should be to build an intelligent urban safety platform that understands how a city behaves, identifies environmental risks, helps people make informed travel decisions, and connects them with assistance when needed.**

Think of it as combining:

- Google Maps-style navigation.
- Urban safety intelligence.
- Real-time city activity monitoring.
- Community-powered reporting.
- Emergency assistance.
- Predictive environmental risk analysis.
- Civic infrastructure analytics.

And I would not restrict it to women-only navigation. Women should remain the primary design focus, but the underlying platform could eventually support children, elderly people, night-shift workers, pedestrians and other vulnerable commuters.

There is one important architectural principle I want to establish from the beginning:

**Never claim that an algorithm knows whether a street is safe. It can estimate observable risk indicators, communicate uncertainty and recommend better-informed alternatives.**

That distinction will matter enormously for the credibility of this product.

### **A. Street safety is multidimensional**

A 2025 systematic literature review on women's perceived safety in urban public spaces identifies individual, social, environmental and temporal factors. It also highlights the importance of looking beyond a simple day-versus-night distinction.&#x20;

ScienceDirect



A separate 2023 study on pedestrian route choice found that the built environment, mixed land use and time of day influence route preferences.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.sciencedirect.com\&sz=32)

ScienceDirect



This means our scoring model should not just count businesses.

It should consider:

- Street lighting.
- Pedestrian infrastructure.
- Visibility and sightlines.
- Active commercial frontage.
- Road isolation.
- Public transport availability.
- Construction and road closures.
- Time-specific establishment activity.
- Verified incident reports.
- Access to assistance.

I would seriously consider Valhalla as the routing foundation.

Its architecture supports pedestrian, bicycle, automobile and multimodal routing, with dynamic costing. This is particularly valuable because we can customize route preferences without maintaining a completely separate road graph for every type of journey.&#x20;

### **Database architecture**

PostGIS should become the heart of the system.

We need more than a table containing latitude, longitude and a safety score.

I would separate the database into these logical schemas:

| Schema           | Tables / entities                                                |
| ---------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `geography`      | cities, wards, roads, road_segments, intersections               |
| `infrastructure` | streetlights, cameras, hospitals, police stations, transit stops |
| `businesses`     | establishments, opening_hours, activity_observations             |
| `incidents`      | reports, verified_incidents, incident_categories                 |
| `temporal`       | hourly_activity, historical_conditions                           |
| `intelligence`   | segment_features, risk_estimates, confidence_scores              |

### **Layer 4: Confidence intelligence**

This is something I would absolutely add.

Suppose two roads both receive an estimated environmental score of 70.

- Road A has 300 recent observations.
- Road B has 3 observations from two years ago.

Those scores should not be presented with equal confidence.

Every intelligence estimate should have:

`estimate + confidence + freshness + evidence_sources`

The application should visibly say when data is sparse, outdated or unavailable.

## **5. Rebuild the route scoring algorithm**

Instead of calculating a score for an entire route using only a few nearby POIs, score individual road segments.

Let:

- \\(E\\) = environmental conditions.
- \\(A\\) = estimated local activity.
- \\(I\\) = verified incident evidence.
- \\(T\\) = transport availability.
- \\(H\\) = access to assistance.
- \\(P\\) = pedestrian infrastructure.
- \\(C\\) = confidence in available data.

An initial experimental model could be:

\\[ S_e = 0.25E + 0.15A + 0.15T + 0.15H + 0.20P + 0.10I_s \\]

Here \\(I_s\\) is an incident-related score where greater verified risk evidence reduces the score.

This is a starting hypothesis, not a validated safety formula. The weights must be calibrated using field observations and user research.

### **Introduce three different scores**

**1. Environmental Support Score**

# **76/100**

How favorable are the observable environmental conditions?

**2. Incident Evidence Indicator**

# **Moderate**

What verified incident evidence is available for this area and time window?

**3. Data Confidence**

# **84/100**

How complete, recent and reliable is the underlying evidence?

Illustrative UI values only. These are not measurements or validated predictions.

This is much more honest and informative than a single number called "Safety: 76".

### **Route optimization**

I would generate several route candidates, then score them.

For example:

1. Fastest route.
2. Best environmental-support route.
3. Best pedestrian-infrastructure route.
4. Balanced route.
5. Public-transport-assisted route.

The user can compare them.

The route optimizer should consider:

\\[ Cost(R) = \alpha \cdot Time(R) + \beta \cdot Distance(R) + \gamma \cdot EnvironmentalPenalty(R) + \delta \cdot IncidentPenalty(R) \\]

We should also enforce hard constraints:

- No inaccessible pedestrian paths.
- No prohibited road access.
- No known closed routes.
- No disconnected route segments.

Valhalla's dynamic costing architecture is a strong fit for this design.&#x20;

- Ingestion timestamp.
- Observation timestamp.
- Geographic accuracy.
- License.
- Verification status.
- Expiry policy.

This prevents the classic problem where nobody knows where a particular map marker or intelligence score originated.

## **8. AI and machine learning: where it actually belongs**

I would avoid putting a generative AI model in the middle of the routing engine.

LLMs are not reliable numerical safety estimators.

Instead, use conventional ML for structured prediction, geospatial algorithms for route analysis and LLMs only for language-oriented tasks.

### **Proposed AI subsystems**

**1. Environmental classification**

High priority

Detect infrastructure conditions using verified imagery and field observations.

Potential models: computer vision classifiers and segmentation models.

**2. Temporal activity estimation**

High priority

Estimate expected business activity and infrastructure availability by location and time.

Potential models: LightGBM, temporal regression and time-series forecasting.

**3. Anomaly detection**

Medium priority

Detect unusual changes in reports, infrastructure status and environmental observations.

Potential models: Isolation Forest and statistical change detection.

**4. Report intelligence**

Medium priority

Classify submitted reports, identify duplicates and detect spam.

Potential models: multilingual text classifiers and moderation pipelines.

**5. Natural language assistant**

Later

Allow users to ask questions about routes, nearby facilities and journey options.

An LLM can explain existing evidence, but cannot invent safety claims.

### **A particularly interesting feature: Street Digital Twins**

Eventually, each road segment could have a digital representation.

Not a photorealistic 3D city, but a continuously updated collection of attributes:

```
StreetSegment
 ├── Geometry
 ├── RoadClassification
 ├── PedestrianInfrastructure
 ├── LightingInventory
 ├── BusinessActivity
 ├── TransportConnectivity
 ├── VerifiedObservations
 ├── HistoricalConditions
 ├── EnvironmentalEstimates
 ├── IncidentEvidence
 └── DataConfidence
```

This digital representation becomes the common intelligence layer used by navigation, civic analytics, emergency assistance and prediction.

It is also reusable for other urban applications.

### **Computer vision opportunities**

Later, we could use street-level images to identify:

- Broken streetlights.
- Missing sidewalks.
- Damaged pedestrian crossings.
- Road obstructions.
- Poorly maintained infrastructure.
- Visible lighting conditions.

However, imagery collection must be lawful, privacy-preserving and appropriately licensed. Avoid facial identification, identifying individuals or attempting to infer criminal behavior from appearances.

There is also an important research caveat about CCTV. Evidence reviewed by the UK College of Policing indicates that CCTV may reduce certain types of crime, but does not establish effectiveness against violent crime specifically related to violence against women and girls. Improved street lighting has stronger general crime-reduction evidence, but that is not proof that a particular route is safe.&#x20;

## **10. Security and privacy architecture**

This product will handle exceptionally sensitive information.

Location history, journey destinations, emergency events and incident reports must be treated as sensitive operational data.

I would implement:

| Control           | Implementation                     |
| ----------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Authentication    | OAuth/OIDC and secure sessions     |
| Authorization     | RBAC with resource-level checks    |
| Location sharing  | Explicit consent and expiry        |
| Data encryption   | TLS and encrypted storage          |
| Sensitive logs    | No raw location coordinates        |
| Retention         | Purpose-specific retention periods |
| Deletion          | User-accessible deletion workflows |
| Emergency access  | Audited and restricted             |
| Community reports | Pseudonymous public identities     |
| Abuse prevention  | Rate limits and moderation         |

The system should separate personal journey records from aggregated urban intelligence.

For example, the route analysis engine should be able to learn about a street without receiving a person's identity or retaining their complete journey history.

We should also perform a privacy impact assessment before introducing any large-scale location tracking or predictive surveillance features.

## **13. How do we know whether it actually works?**

We need an evaluation framework from the beginning.

A route that receives a high algorithmic score isn't automatically a successful route.

I would establish these KPIs:

| Category        | Metric                                          |
| --------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Navigation      | Route generation latency                        |
| Navigation      | Route completion rate                           |
| Data            | POI and infrastructure freshness                |
| Data            | Percentage of segments with sufficient evidence |
| Intelligence    | Prediction calibration                          |
| Intelligence    | Field-observation agreement                     |
| Community       | Report verification rate                        |
| Emergency       | SOS workflow reliability                        |
| Privacy         | Unauthorized location-access incidents          |
| User experience | User-reported route suitability                 |

### **Ground-truth validation**

Before calling the model predictive, conduct structured field audits.

For each sampled road segment, trained auditors should independently record:

- Lighting conditions.
- Sidewalk continuity.
- Visibility.
- Public activity.
- Nearby assistance.
- Temporary obstructions.

Collect feedback from diverse participants, particularly women travelling at different times and using different modes of transportation.

Evaluate the system separately across neighborhoods, times and user groups. A model that performs well in commercial areas but poorly in residential neighborhoods should not be considered successful.

# **16. Five research directions I don't want us to overlook**

These are potentially the most valuable areas for further technical exploration.

Research priority 1

**Human perception versus measurable environmental conditions**

Can we model the difference between what makes a street objectively well-equipped and what makes different people feel comfortable walking there?

Research priority 2

**Street-level computer vision**

Can we automatically identify infrastructure deficiencies using properly licensed imagery and privacy-preserving vision models?

Research priority 3

**Spatiotemporal graph intelligence**

Can we model the entire city as a dynamic graph where the attributes and availability of individual road segments change over time?

Research priority 4

**Federated urban intelligence**

Can multiple campuses, RWAs and organizations contribute infrastructure information without sharing their private operational databases?

Research priority 5

**Causal evaluation of infrastructure improvements**

Can we measure whether installing streetlights, repairing footpaths or improving transport access actually changes reported experiences and observed conditions?

So far, we've designed a platform that collects urban data, calculates environmental indicators and recommends routes.

But there's a much bigger opportunity:

**Build an Urban Intelligence Operating System that continuously learns how a city works, understands its infrastructure, detects changes, and helps people and institutions respond.**

The navigation application should just be one interface into that system.

I see six opportunities to make it substantially more advanced:

1. Make the city a continuously updated, queryable digital twin.
2. Introduce a much smarter geospatial intelligence engine.
3. Build a data network that improves as more institutions participate.
4. Introduce predictive infrastructure and mobility intelligence.
5. Build a proper real-time operational system.
6. Make the platform independently measurable, explainable and trustworthy.

Let's take it beyond the conventional smart-city architecture.

## **1. Upgrade the digital twin into a living city model**

The previous architecture had a digital twin containing road segments and infrastructure attributes.

I'd make it much more sophisticated.

Instead of treating a road as a static object, model it as an entity with:

- Physical properties.
- Connected infrastructure.
- Time-dependent conditions.
- Historical observations.
- Current operational status.
- Predicted future conditions.
- Relationships with nearby entities.

### **Introduce a City Entity Graph**

Imagine this:

```
CITY
 ├── ZONE
 │    ├── NEIGHBORHOOD
 │    │    ├── ROAD SEGMENT
 │    │    │    ├── Streetlights
 │    │    │    ├── Footpaths
 │    │    │    ├── Crossings
 │    │    │    ├── Businesses
 │    │    │    ├── Transit stops
 │    │    │    ├── Public facilities
 │    │    │    └── Observations
 │    │    │
 │    │    └── BUILDINGS
 │    │         ├── Hospitals
 │    │         ├── Campuses
 │    │         └── Commercial buildings
 │    │
 │    └── TRANSPORT NETWORK
 │         ├── Metro stations
 │         ├── Bus stops
 │         └── Transit connections
```

Every entity has relationships with other entities.

For example:

A streetlight belongs to a road segment. That road segment connects to an intersection. The intersection connects to multiple streets. A business is accessible from one or more street segments.

Now we can answer much more interesting questions:

- Which streets have no functioning streetlights within a certain distance?
- Which residential areas have poor walking connectivity to public transport?
- Which hospitals are accessible from a particular neighbourhood?
- Which pedestrian routes have several infrastructure deficiencies?
- Which areas have not been physically verified for six months?

### **Add temporal versions**

This is particularly important.

A streetlight might be installed today, fail next month and get repaired two days later.

We need two timestamps:

`valid_time`: When the information was true in the real world.

`recorded_time`: When our system learned about it.

This allows historical reconstruction and prevents outdated information from silently overwriting newer evidence.

The Open Geospatial Consortium's urban digital twin research specifically discusses the challenge of integrating persistent infrastructure models with dynamic observations and multiple interoperable digital twins.&#x20;

**My recommendation:** Use PostGIS as the authoritative spatial store, an event history for changes, and a graph projection for relationship-heavy queries. Don't immediately introduce Neo4j just because we're building a graph. PostgreSQL can handle a surprising amount of this.

## **2. Build a multi-layer intelligence engine**

Currently, we have an environmental intelligence engine.

I would turn it into a collection of specialized engines.

# **Urban Intelligence Core**

Proposed independently deployable analytical modules

**Mobility Intelligence**

Connectivity, accessibility and route quality

**Infrastructure Intelligence**

Lighting, footpaths and facilities

**Temporal Intelligence**

Hourly and seasonal conditions

**Activity Intelligence**

Estimated activity and availability

**Incident Intelligence**

Verified evidence and trends

**Environmental Intelligence**

Weather, heat, flooding and visibility

**Accessibility Intelligence**

Universal pedestrian access

**Predictive Intelligence**

Forecasts and scenario analysis

### **A new capability: Cross-layer reasoning**

This is more valuable than eight independent scores.

Consider a hypothetical street:

- Good street lighting.
- Several businesses.
- No continuous footpath.
- Heavy rain forecast.
- A nearby underpass prone to flooding.

A basic model might rank this route highly because it has good lighting and activity.

A cross-layer model recognizes that flooding and missing pedestrian infrastructure may make the route unsuitable.

The engines should therefore communicate through a shared feature model.

For example:

```
RouteCandidate
 ├── MobilityAssessment
 ├── InfrastructureAssessment
 ├── ActivityAssessment
 ├── WeatherAssessment
 ├── AccessibilityAssessment
 ├── IncidentEvidence
 ├── DataConfidence
 └── RecommendationExplanation
```

This is how we move from feature accumulation to actual intelligence.

## **3. Introduce a Route Intelligence Lab**

I would make routing a much bigger subsystem.

Instead of returning three routes, give users a way to understand the trade-offs.

[coreoperationsforged.com](https://coreoperationsforged.com/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### **Multi-objective route optimization**

The system should optimize multiple objectives simultaneously:

- Travel time.
- Distance.
- Walking infrastructure.
- Lighting evidence.
- Emergency assistance accessibility.
- Transit availability.
- Environmental disruptions.
- User accessibility requirements.

Rather than compressing everything into one weighted score, use **Pareto optimization**.

A route is Pareto-dominated when another route is at least as good in every selected objective and strictly better in at least one.

This gives us a set of meaningful alternatives instead of pretending that one universal weighting works for everybody.

### **Add route explanations**

For every recommendation, show:

**Why was this route recommended?**

- Better pedestrian infrastructure.
- More verified lighting coverage.
- More available public facilities.
- Fewer known infrastructure disruptions.

**What are its disadvantages?**

- Four minutes longer.
- One additional crossing.
- Limited recent observations on one segment.

This makes the recommendation inspectable.

### **Add a route simulator**

This could be an especially powerful feature.

Allow users to change the conditions:

- What if a road is closed?
- What if the metro station is unavailable?
- What if the weather changes?
- What if a streetlight is reported broken?
- What if a business closes earlier than expected?

The system recalculates route alternatives.

For routing, Valhalla already supports time-dependent routing, dynamic runtime costing and multimodal journeys. That gives us a strong starting point.&#x20;

### **Introduce an observation protocol**

Every observation should be a first-class object.

```
{
  "observation_id": "OBS-0001",
  "entity_id": "ROAD-001",
  "observation_type": "streetlight_status",
  "observed_value": "not_working",
  "observed_at": "2026-11-12T20:30:00+05:30",
  "source_type": "field_audit",
  "verification_status": "pending",
  "confidence": 0.0,
  "expires_at": null
}
```

The confidence here is deliberately not assigned a fabricated value. It should be calculated after source validation.

### **Introduce evidence fusion**

Suppose we receive:

1. A municipal maintenance record.
2. A field auditor's observation.
3. Three independent community submissions.

The system can combine these sources, taking into account their reliability, timestamps, independence and geographic accuracy.

However, three copies of the same original report should not count as three independent confirmations.

That requires source lineage and duplicate detection.

## **5. Introduce a Real-Time City Operations Layer**

Here's something I would add that wasn't properly covered in the previous design.

The platform should understand not just the city, but what is changing in the city right now.

For that, introduce an event-driven architecture.

### **Urban Event Bus**

Every important change generates an event.

Examples:

```
streetlight.reported_broken
streetlight.verified_broken
streetlight.repaired

road.closure_detected
road.closure_confirmed
road.reopened

business.opening_hours_changed
transit.service_disrupted
weather.flood_warning
community.report_submitted
emergency.session_started
```

These events flow through an event-processing layer.

### **What this enables**

Imagine a road closure is verified.

The platform can automatically:

1. Update the affected road segment.
2. Recalculate affected routes.
3. Notify users who opted into relevant journey alerts.
4. Update the city operations dashboard.
5. Preserve the previous state for historical analysis.
6. Trigger an investigation if the closure has no known expiry.

This is much more powerful than periodically refreshing a database.

### **Technology choice**

I would begin with Redis Streams for smaller-scale event processing.

When we have multiple cities, many independent consumers and significant event volume, consider Redpanda or Kafka.

Do not introduce Kafka simply because it looks impressive in an architecture diagram.

Instead of only showing where infrastructure is currently deficient, predict where maintenance or intervention may be needed.

| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |

For example, suppose a group of streetlights repeatedly fails.

The platform could identify:

- Geographic concentration of failures.
- Average repair time.
- Repeat maintenance frequency.
- Nearby streets affected.
- Maintenance backlog.

The output would be a maintenance priority recommendation, not an assertion that a street is dangerous.

### **A particularly useful addition: Intervention Simulator**

Give municipal authorities the ability to simulate proposed improvements.

For example:

**Scenario A:** Install 20 additional streetlights.

**Scenario B:** Repair three broken pedestrian crossings.

**Scenario C:** Extend a bus service by one hour.

The platform estimates how each proposal changes:

- Infrastructure coverage.
- Pedestrian connectivity.
- Access to public facilities.
- Route options.
- Accessibility gaps.

This is where the digital twin becomes useful for decision-making.

We should distinguish modelled scenario outcomes from measured real-world improvements. The latter require before-and-after evaluation.

A useful example:

A user submits a photograph of a damaged footpath.

The pipeline:

1. Checks file validity and strips unnecessary metadata.
2. Detects whether the image contains relevant infrastructure.
3. Classifies the reported defect.
4. Compares it with existing reports.
5. Associates it with a probable road segment.
6. Sends it for verification.
7. Updates infrastructure status after verification.

No image should automatically become authoritative evidence merely because an AI model classified it.

### **Introduce model governance**

Every deployed model should have:

- Model version.
- Training data provenance.
- Evaluation results.
- Known limitations.
- Geographic performance breakdown.
- Drift monitoring.
- Rollback capability.

And we should explicitly prohibit models that infer criminality, suspiciousness or threat from people's appearance, ethnicity, gender or ordinary movement patterns.

## **14. Five things I would deliberately NOT build**

Sometimes improving architecture means deciding what not to implement.

1. **No universal safety score.** Different people have different needs, and environmental indicators are not proof of personal safety.
2. **No permanent location tracking.** Journey intelligence should work with minimum necessary personal data.
3. **No unverified crime heatmaps.** Poorly sourced data can stigmatize entire neighbourhoods.
4. **No unnecessary microservices.** Modular monolith first, extract services when scale or operational isolation justifies it.
5. **No AI-generated emergency decisions.** Emergency workflows should be deterministic, tested and reliable.

# **15. My biggest recommendation: build three technological moats**

If we want this to become a serious platform, I would concentrate on three things that are difficult to replicate.

Moat 1

# **Urban Knowledge Graph**

A continuously updated, provenance-aware representation of roads, infrastructure, businesses, services and their relationships.

Moat 2

# **Temporal Urban Intelligence**

An understanding of how the same physical location changes across hours, days, seasons and real-world events.

Moat 3

# **Verified Observation Network**

A growing network of participating institutions, field auditors, community contributors and authorized data providers.

These three capabilities would be much more defensible than having a particularly attractive map UI or a chatbot.

## **9. Build a Data Quality and Evidence Engine**

This might be more valuable than introducing another AI model.

The application needs to know not just what it knows, but how it knows it.

## **Evidence Intelligence**

Every data item should carry:

- Source and source license.
- Original source identifier.
- Observation timestamp.
- Ingestion timestamp.
- Geographic precision.
- Verification status.
- Confidence methodology.
- Validity period.
- Duplicate relationships.
- Correction history.

### **Introduce evidence classes**

| Class               | Meaning                                |
| ------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| Official            | Published by an authorized institution |
| Field verified      | Checked by a trained auditor           |
| Partner verified    | Confirmed by an approved organization  |
| Community confirmed | Independently corroborated reports     |
| Community reported  | Not yet verified                       |
| Inferred            | Estimated by a model                   |
| Unknown             | No reliable information available      |

Don't automatically rank all official data above every field observation. Official records can also be stale.

The platform should evaluate the source, recency, precision and relevance of each observation.

### **Add an evidence expiry engine**

Different types of information should have different validity policies.

For example:

- Business opening hours: refresh periodically.
- Road closure: expire or revalidate quickly.
- Streetlight inventory: review periodically.
- Streetlight failure report: revalidate after maintenance.
- Hospital location: relatively stable, but operating availability may change.
- Weather: rapidly expiring.

This avoids a database full of historical facts masquerading as live information.

### **New capability: Maintenance Management**

This is a big one.

When someone reports a broken streetlight:

1. NightWise creates an observation.
2. The system identifies the associated infrastructure asset.
3. An authorized reviewer validates the report.
4. The responsible organization receives a maintenance request.
5. The organization updates the status.
6. The repair is independently verified.
7. The infrastructure record is updated.

This is a complete feedback loop.

The application is no longer just telling people which streets have problems. It can help organizations fix them.

### **Example scenarios**

**Scenario A: Lighting improvement**

Add 30 proposed streetlights to selected roads.

- Change in mapped lighting coverage.
- Number of affected road segments.
- Nearby pedestrian connections.
- Infrastructure cost estimates if supplied.

**Scenario B: Pedestrian connectivity**

Add a proposed footpath connection.

Calculate:

- Previously disconnected pedestrian paths.
- New accessible routes.
- Connectivity to transit.
- Affected neighbourhoods.

**Scenario C: Public transport improvement**

Extend the operating window of a verified transport service.

Calculate:

- Newly available journey options.
- Areas gaining last-mile connectivity.

Model the impact of a proposed drainage or road elevation improvement using suitable terrain and hydrological data.

## **18. Upgrade the routing engine beyond weighted scoring**

The current route formula is a reasonable prototype, but I would change the optimizer itself.

Use a two-stage architecture.

**Stage 1: Candidate generation**

Generate diverse route candidates using the routing engine.

- Fastest.
- Shortest.
- Better pedestrian infrastructure.
- More available public facilities.
- Accessible.
- Transit-assisted.

  **Stage 2: Multi-objective evaluation**

Evaluate each route using segment-level information, hard constraints and the user's preferences.

Remove dominated options, explain trade-offs and rank the remaining candidates.

### **Introduce Pareto-optimal routing**

This is an important algorithmic improvement.

Suppose we have three routes:

| Route | Time   | Accessibility | Lighting evidence |
| ----- | ------ | ------------- | ----------------- |
| A     | 10 min | Poor          | Limited           |
| B     | 13 min | Good          | Good              |
| C     | 15 min | Good          | Limited           |

Route B may be a better overall compromise than A or C.

But we shouldn't automatically declare B safest. Users should be able to inspect the individual trade-offs.

### **Introduce hard constraints**

Some conditions shouldn't be treated as merely another weighted preference.

Examples:

- Inaccessible paths for a wheelchair profile.
- Legally prohibited pedestrian roads.
- Confirmed road closures.
- Disconnected footpaths.
- Known transit service cancellation.

If a route violates a hard constraint, reject it rather than compensate with a high score elsewhere.

### **Add a route uncertainty penalty**

A route passing through poorly documented infrastructure should be clearly marked as having limited information.

Don't silently equate unknown conditions with good conditions.

This is a particularly important design decision for under-mapped areas.

# **Part IX. Additional research directions**

There are still several research opportunities I would keep open for a longer-term R&D programme.

| Research area                | Potential contribution                            |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Graph Neural Networks        | Learn spatial relationships between road segments |
| Spatiotemporal forecasting   | Estimate changes in infrastructure and activity   |
| Computer vision              | Automate pedestrian infrastructure assessment     |
| Multi-objective optimization | Generate more meaningful route alternatives       |
| Digital twins                | Model existing and proposed city infrastructure   |
| Federated analytics          | Enable partner data collaboration                 |
| Differential privacy         | Protect aggregated mobility statistics            |
| Causal inference             | Evaluate real infrastructure interventions        |
| Human-centred design         | Understand differing perceptions of public space  |
| Multilingual NLP             | Process community reports in Indian languages     |

I would be especially interested in combining temporal graph learning with geospatial infrastructure data.

For instance, the same road network could have different activity, accessibility and environmental attributes depending on the hour, day and weather.

But this should come after establishing a sufficiently reliable dataset. A sophisticated graph neural network trained on unreliable map information will just produce sophisticated nonsense.

**Potential scoring inconsistency in the demo**

The scoring breakdown displays:

| Component   | Points   |
| ----------- | -------- |
| Open places | 14.3     |
| Help points | 7.9      |
| Main roads  | 12.0     |
| Transport   | 5.6      |
| Fewer gaps  | 15.0     |
| Fewer turns | 8.2      |
| **Total**   | **63.0** |

However, the accompanying Night Activity Score indicator displays 14/100.

The route comparison also shows a score of 14 for the selected recommended route and 27 for the fastest route.

These may be different metrics, an animation-state mismatch or a demo-data inconsistency. The implementation should make the relationship explicit and verify that every displayed score reconciles with its underlying formula.

Other things I would audit:

- Are business hours timezone-aware and correct for the actual date?
- Are overnight opening schedules supported?
- Are closed days and special holiday hours considered?
- Are help points actually accessible to the public?
- Are main roads pedestrian-accessible?
- Are route segments properly connected?
- Are multiple reports from the same original source deduplicated?
- Does a missing streetlight record mean no streetlight, or simply no information?
- Does the route comparison use exactly the same scoring model for every candidate?
- Can the route be physically walked?

These are small-looking details that can make or break the entire product.

## **What I would prioritize above everything else**

My top five investments would be:

1. **Street-level intelligence:** Understand every individual road segment, not just entire routes.
2. **Evidence infrastructure:** Know the source, accuracy, freshness and verification status of every data point.
3. **Pedestrian accessibility:** Make actual walkability a first-class part of navigation.
4. **Real-time urban changes:** Let verified observations update the system.
5. **Institutional feedback loops:** Help organizations identify and resolve infrastructure deficiencies.

These create a foundation that can support every other feature.

## **One thing I want to establish before we start coding**

I would like to treat this as a proper engineering programme, with a versioned architecture specification rather than continuing to add features informally.

The next deliverable should be a **NightWise V2 Master Technical Specification**, containing:

- Complete system architecture.
- Database schemas and PostGIS models.
- Service boundaries.
- API contracts.
- Routing algorithms.
- Intelligence scoring methodology.
- Data-source catalogue and licensing.
- ML pipelines and evaluation.
- Emergency workflows.
- Privacy and security architecture.
- Repository structure.
- Deployment topology.
- Testing and validation strategy.
- Phased implementation tickets.

My recommendation is to make the geospatial and evidence foundation the first implementation milestone. Everything else should build on it.

The ambition should be substantial, but the system must earn every intelligence claim through real data and validation. That's how NightWise can become something genuinely useful, rather than another impressive-looking map.
